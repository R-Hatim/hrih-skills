# Research and angle

Generic copy usually comes from a thin brief rather than from weak sentences. Polishing generic copy only gets you smoother generic copy. This file covers how to get the raw material before you write: customer language, positioning, the reader's awareness, and frameworks used as checks.

Scale it to the job. A one-line button needs none of this. A homepage or launch email needs most of it, even if research means 15 minutes with the material the user already has.

## 1. The brief

Fill what you can from the conversation and the project. Ask only for gaps that would change the copy.

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What it is, in one plain sentence | If you can't say it, the headline can't either |
| Reader: who, their situation, what triggered this visit | Sets awareness level and vocabulary |
| Where they come from (ad, search, email, in-app, cold) | The first line must continue the promise that brought them |
| The one action, and what happens after it | Drives the CTA and the reassurance next to it |
| What they'd use instead (a competitor, a spreadsheet, hiring someone, doing nothing) | The real comparison your copy has to win |
| Differentiators *with evidence* | Only proven differentiators make it into copy |
| Proof inventory: numbers with sources, named customers, quotes with permission, ratings, guarantees, policies | The only allowed source of specifics |
| Objections and anxieties | Feed FAQ, CTA microcopy, risk reversal |
| Voice: samples, links, words to use or avoid | Calibrate from samples, not adjectives |
| Limits: character counts, legal or regulated claims, locale, reading level | Hard constraints, checked last |

## 2. Customer language (voice of customer)

The best copy is assembled from the words customers already use. Customers describe the problem in plainer and more vivid terms than any marketer does, and readers recognize their own words.

**Sources, best first.** Use what the user gives you and public pages. Never log in anywhere.
1. Interviews, sales or support call notes, support tickets, cancellation reasons, survey and NPS comments.
2. Reviews of the product, its competitors, and other ways people solve the same problem (G2, Capterra, app stores, Amazon, Trustpilot, Reddit, forums).
   - Read mostly the 2-, 3- and 4-star reviews; they're the most honest.
   - Skim the 1s and 5s.
   - Clip phrases that stop you; don't summarize while reading.
   - Discount reviews that look fake.
3. The user's own best-performing copy and the words they use when they talk about the product.

**Tag each snippet:**

| Tag | Capture | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | The situation, in their words | Hero subhead, problem section, cold-email opener |
| Consequence | What it costs them: time, money, status, sleep | The "before" that makes the change worth it |
| Trigger | The moment they started looking | Ad hooks, email timing |
| Current alternative | What they use or do today | Positioning, comparison sections |
| Desired outcome | The "after", in their words | Headline candidates |
| Objection / anxiety | Fears about switching, price, effort, risk | FAQ, CTA microcopy, guarantees |
| Habit | What keeps them stuck | Migration and onboarding copy |
| Vivid phrase | Words or images that stop you | Headlines, subject lines |

**Count repetition.** Phrases that recur set the message order. Vivid one-offs become headline candidates.

**Switching forces** (from Jobs-to-be-Done):
- A person switches when the push of the current pain plus the pull of the new outcome outweigh their anxiety about the new thing plus their habits with the old one.
- Most copy only does "pull". Good copy also names the push, and lowers anxiety and habit: migration help, keeps your data, cancel anytime (if true).

**Rules:**
- A review is research, not a testimonial. Quote real customers only with the user's go-ahead.
- If you have no customer material and can't get any, say so, write from the brief, and mark lines that would improve with research: `[VOC: how customers describe the problem]`.

## 3. Positioning

Work out positioning in this order (April Dunford's sequence). Each step depends on the one before.
1. **Competitive alternatives**: what customers would do if this product didn't exist.
2. **Unique attributes**: what this product has that those alternatives lack.
3. **Value**: what those attributes let customers do, with proof.
4. **Target customers**: who cares most about that value.
5. **Category**: the frame that makes the value obvious ("scheduling for independent cafés").

Positioning is the context the copy works in, not a tagline. A one-line summary helps you think: *[category] for [target] that [unique value], unlike [alternative].* It rarely belongs in the copy word for word.

**A point of view helps.** A stance a reasonable person could disagree with makes copy distinctive, especially for founder-led pages, launches and posts. Grounding it in experience separates it from contrarianism for show. It must be the user's real view. Don't invent opinions for them.

## 4. Awareness and market sophistication

**Awareness** (from Eugene Schwartz) sets how the copy opens and how long it needs to be:

| The reader… | Open with | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Knows the product and wants it | The offer: price, terms, button | Shortest |
| Knows the product, isn't convinced | Proof, differences, comparisons, risk reversal | Short to medium |
| Knows this kind of solution, not you | Your mechanism: why this way works | Medium |
| Knows the problem, not the solutions | The problem in their words, then the way out | Medium to long |
| Doesn't see the problem yet | A story, a surprising fact, or a situation they recognize ("If you run a three-person agency…") | Longest; often content rather than a sales page |

Copy can't create desire; it can only channel a desire the reader already has toward the product. Find that desire in the research instead of manufacturing it.

**Market sophistication** asks how many similar claims this market has already heard:
- **New market:** say the claim simply.
- **Crowded with the same claim:** make it bigger and more specific, or explain the *mechanism* that makes it true.
- **Jaded market:** identification often works better than claims: who the product is for and how they see themselves.

Most software categories are crowded. "Save time" is invisible there; how it saves time, or exactly whom it's built for, isn't.

## 5. Message hierarchy

Before drafting, decide:
- **One big idea**: the headline's job.
- **3–5 supporting points**, ranked by how much customers care, each paired with its proof.
- **The top 2–3 objections** and their answers.
- **The CTA** and the reassurance next to it.

## 6. Frameworks: when to use, when not

Frameworks describe the order of an argument. Filled in mechanically, they produce the same recognizable template on every page. Use them to check that nothing is missing, then write from the real material in the real voice.

| Framework | Use when | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| **PAS** (problem, agitate, solution) | Problem-aware readers; short emails, ads, hero sections, cold email | Agitation turns into melodrama or fear. Wrong for readers who don't feel the pain yet, and for readers who just want the deal. Never on error, billing or support surfaces |
| **BAB** (before, after, bridge) | Transformation stories, case studies, welcome and onboarding emails, posts | The "after" must be concrete and believable. "Imagine a world where…" is a tell |
| **AIDA** (attention, interest, desire, action) | Checking that a long page or email moves from hook to action | Too general to guide sentences. "Attention" becomes clickbait if it isn't tied to the offer |
| **4 Ps** (promise, picture, proof, push) | Direct-response pages and launch emails with strong proof | Without real proof, the promise becomes hype |
| **Features → advantages → benefits**, and the "so what?" ladder | Feature sections, product descriptions, B2B, release notes | Climb until the reader cares, then stop one rung before it turns abstract ("…so you can be happier"). Keep the plain feature visible for technical buyers |
| **4 U's** (useful, urgent, unique, ultra-specific) | Scoring headline and subject-line candidates | A filter, not a generator. Add urgency only if it's real |
| **Rule of one** (one reader, one idea, one promise, one action) | Every landing page, email and ad | Homepages serving several audiences need routing, not one idea crammed for everyone |
| **Slippery slide** (each sentence gets the next one read) | Long sales copy, emails, scripts | Not a license for curiosity gaps the copy never pays off |
| **"Claim, even if objection"** ("Set up in an afternoon, even if your data lives in five spreadsheets") | Subheads, feature heads, CTA microcopy | Once or twice per page; repeated, it becomes a tic |
| **Three questions** (can I picture it? could it be proven false? could only we say it?) | Every headline and claim | Transitional lines and CTA microcopy can just be plain |

**Editing sweeps** (based on Joanna Wiebe's Seven Sweeps), for longer copy, in this order:
1. Clarity.
2. Voice and tone.
3. So what?
4. Prove it.
5. Specificity.
6. Honest emotion: the "before" is felt, in customer words, without manufactured fear.
7. Zero risk: next to each CTA, say what happens next, the cost, the commitment, and how to cancel.

After fixing one sweep, glance back at the earlier ones; fixes can break them. SKILL.md step 4 folds these into its passes.
