## 1. Rules for every email

- **One email, one job, one primary CTA.** A secondary link is fine; a second ask is not.
- **Sender:** a real person ("Maya from Streakly") for welcome, onboarding, founder notes and cold email, when that's true. The brand for receipts and transactional mail.
- **Open mid-conversation.** No "I hope this email finds you well", no "We're excited to announce". The first line shows in many inbox previews, so make it carry information.
- **Short paragraphs.** One idea each, scannable on a phone. Put the CTA after the value is clear. For a designed email, use a button plus a text link.
- **Write to one person.** "You", their situation, what they get. Count "you/your" against "we/our": in most email, "you" should win.
- **Personalization only when it's real.** Merge fields need fallbacks (`{first_name|there}`), and a wrong name is worse than none.
- **No Markdown** unless you know the email tool renders it. Deliver subject, preview text and body as separate labelled parts.

## 2. Subject line and preview text

- **Clear beats clever; specific beats vague.**
  - Front-load the meaningful words, because mobile clients cut subjects off.
  - About 30–50 characters survives most clients.
- **Write 5+ candidates** across approaches (outcome, news, question in the reader's words, specific number, personal note), then pick.
- **Never:**
  - a fake "Re:" or "Fwd:"
  - bait the body doesn't deliver
  - ALL CAPS
  - a string of emoji
- **Preview text** continues the subject; it doesn't repeat it, and it shouldn't waste space on "View in browser". Put the payload in the first ~40–90 characters, since clients show very different lengths.
- **Newsletters:** sell the best item in this issue, not "Issue #42".

## 3. Welcome email (sent right after signup)

Jobs, in order:
1. Deliver what was promised (login link, download, discount code).
2. Confirm they're in the right place.
3. Set expectations: what you'll send and how often.
4. One next step toward the first useful result.

Don't pitch an upgrade yet. Keep it short. A personal sender and a plain style usually work well here.

## 4. Onboarding sequence

- **Each email pushes one activation step** toward the moment the product first proves useful (the first habit created, first invoice sent, first report run).
- **Trigger by behavior where possible,** and stop sending a step's email once the user has done it.
- **Find where new users stall** (support tickets, FAQs, product analytics) and write emails for those points.
- **Pace like a dinner party** (Val Geisler's model): welcome guests before serving the main course.
  1. Welcome and the promised thing.
  2. A small easy win, plus who you are and why.
  3. The core use case, shown through a customer outcome (real ones only).
  4. Something genuinely helpful that isn't selling.
  5. The offer or upgrade, once they've had value.
- **Space emails a few days apart,** and hold back promotional campaigns while someone is in the sequence.

## 5. Launch or announcement email

- The subject says what's new *for the reader*: "You can now export to Xero" beats "Introducing Exports 2.0".
- Lead with the reader's benefit and one concrete example. Include one screenshot or GIF if the email is designed.
- One CTA to try it. Put the changelog details behind a link.
- Skip "We're thrilled/excited to announce". Readers care what changed for them, not how the team feels.

## 6. Newsletter

- **A consistent promise:** what readers get every issue.
- **Lead with the strongest item.** Make each section scannable, with a first sentence that carries the point.
- **Keep the voice personal** and one person's when possible. Take opinions and specifics over link roundups with one-line summaries.
- **CTAs can be soft** (reply, share). A sponsor or promo gets one clearly labelled block.

## 7. Cold outreach (B2B)

- **About them, not you.** Open with a relevant, specific observation or trigger (a hire, a launch, a public post, their stack). If you can delete the personal line and the email still works, it wasn't personal.
- **Short.** In one large 2024 analysis, emails of 20–39 words got the highest average reply rate, though the differences between lengths were small. Cut until cutting would lose information.
- **Shape:**
  1. Observation.
  2. The problem it usually creates.
  3. Proof: a similar company and a real result, only if the user has one.
  4. A low-friction ask ("Worth a look?", "Want the two-minute version?") rather than "30 minutes on Tuesday?".
- **Subject:** 2–4 plain words that look like normal mail, no hype.
- **Follow-ups:** each adds something new (a different angle, proof, a useful resource). Never "just bumping this". Stop on any reply; finish with a polite close-the-loop.
- **Compliance:**
  - an honest sender and subject
  - an opt-out line and a postal address where the law requires them
  - a reminder to check local rules (GDPR, PECR, CAN-SPAM) when the user's market is unclear

## 8. Win-back and re-engagement

An honest check-in, a reminder of value (what's new since they left), an incentive only if one exists, and a clear one-click choice to stay or unsubscribe. No guilt-tripping.

## 9. Plain text vs designed

- **Plain or lightly styled** suits person-to-person, onboarding, founder notes and cold email. It feels like a letter, not a flyer.
- **Designed** suits promotions, visual product launches and newsletters with several items.
- Pick by the relationship and the job, and suggest testing.

## 10. Final checks

- [ ] Subject, preview and body are delivered as separate, labelled parts; the subject is under ~50 characters.
- [ ] One job; one primary CTA; button text says what happens.
- [ ] The first line carries information, not throat-clearing.
- [ ] Every claim, number and name is real or marked `[NEED: …]`; no invented customer stories.
- [ ] Merge fields have fallbacks.
- [ ] No Markdown artifacts; renders as plain text if needed.
- [ ] Cold email: under ~120 words, about them, opt-out where required.
